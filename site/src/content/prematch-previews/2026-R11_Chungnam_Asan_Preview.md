## Strategy That Can Work

The tactical plan should be active rather than simply cautious. Asan's new manager profile points toward collective defending and counters, so Seoul need to make the opponent defend longer sequences while still protecting the first outlet after turnovers.

- Use width to move Asan's compact block before crossing. Early floated service lets Kim Jong-min and the center backs defend facing forward; switches, overlaps, and cutbacks make them turn.
- Keep a true rest-defense triangle behind attacks. Andre's first idea is likely to be cooperative defending into fast counters, so Seoul's holding midfielder and outside center backs must kill the first pass after turnovers.
- Force Asan to chase the game state. Their 2025 losses to Seoul became worse once they had to open up, while the away draw stayed uncomfortable because Seoul never fully broke the match open.
- Attack second balls around set pieces. Seoul's set-piece edge matters most if the first contact is treated as the start of the chance, not the whole chance.

## Keys to a Seoul E-Land Win

### 1. Protect the first pass after turnovers

This is the clearest tactical key. Seoul can live with sterile Asan possession, but they cannot let their own attacks become Asan counters. The center backs and holding midfielders need clean rest-defense spacing whenever the wingbacks or wide forwards push high.

### 2. Create better wide entries, not just more crosses

Asan's transition block is easier to manage if Seoul force it to move first. Switches of play, overlap timing, and cutbacks should be more valuable than early floated balls. The goal is to make Asan defend the second and third pass, not just the first delivery.

### 3. Treat set pieces like a swing factor

Kim Jong-min's aerial profile makes defensive restarts important, but Seoul should also see dead balls as a route to control. A clean set-piece game would reduce Asan's emotional lift and give Seoul a direct way to turn pressure into chances.
